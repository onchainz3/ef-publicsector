Enaleia Hub

Enaleia Hub helped fishers, port coordinators, recyclers, and manufacturers trace marine plastic through the supply chain. It published records on Optimism, an Ethereum layer 2 network, allowing others to review the reported movement of materials.

1.24 million kg of marine waste removed and reported on Enaleia Hub's blockchain-backed dashboard

Institutional context

Marine plastic can pass through fishers, ports, recyclers, and manufacturers before becoming a new product. When records remain in separate spreadsheets and reports, institutions, funders, and other stakeholders may have difficulty tracing materials across the full supply chain. Enaleia works with fishing communities across Greece and the wider Mediterranean to collect marine plastic and used fishing gear and connect suitable materials with recycling and manufacturing partners.

Through Lefteris Arapakis’s 2024 Next Billion Fellowship, Enaleia worked with Pollen Labs to develop a shared tracking system called Enaleia Hub.

System design

At participating ports, coordinators weighed fishers' collections, scanned their identification cards, and recorded the materials using a mobile app. Coordinators submitted records on behalf of collection points, so individual fishers did not need to interact directly with the blockchain. QR labels identified batches as they moved between organizations.

Recyclers recorded incoming weights, sorting, and processing steps such as washing, shredding, and turning plastic into pellets. New batch records linked the materials going into a process with those coming out. Manufacturers could then record production batches associated with finished goods. The app supported offline data entry and submitted records when connectivity returned.

The mobile application is open source, allowing others to inspect, adapt, and reuse the software. A public dashboard presented submitted records as a traceable journey, while a separate backend and administrative tools supported day-to-day operations.

The dashboard also provided product-level traceability. One example was the Nelo 400 Kayak, a kayak manufactured by using a combination of materials, including marine plastic collected through Enaleia's work. Its product record linked the collection, processing, and manufacturing stages.

Enaleia Hub operated from May 2025 to September 2026, and has since closed. Although its public dashboard is no longer operational, the records it published on Optimism remain publicly accessible.

Ethereum’s role

Enaleia Hub used Ethereum Attestation Service on Optimism to publish verifiable records of reported supply-chain events, such as a batch’s material type, weight, and processing stage. These records are known as attestations: digitally signed statements that can be checked independently. Optimism is an Ethereum layer 2 network that processes transactions separately while relying on Ethereum for security.

Each published record identifies the account that submitted it and can be reviewed through publicly accessible tools. Institutions, partners, and outside observers can examine the same records without relying only on Enaleia’s private reporting system. The network preserves what was submitted; checking that materials were actually collected, weighed correctly, and handled as reported still requires checks of the physical process.

Status and milestones

  1. September 2026

    Enaleia Hub closed after operating from May 2025 to September 2026. The historical dashboard reported approximately 1.24 million kg of marine waste removed, including plastics, metals, and fishing gear, with 415,437 kg sorted by recyclers. It reported 4,498 activities across 74 participating locations, including ports, recyclers, and manufacturers.

  2. May 2026

    The Enaleia Hub Android app received an update addressing port selection and other interface controls, providing evidence of continued application maintenance.

  3. May 2025

    Enaleia Hub is deployed on Optimism’s public network and linked directly to its supply-chain records.

Implementation considerations

Effective traceability requires consistent batch identifiers, shared data standards, reliable weighing procedures, and clear responsibility for the information submitted at each stage. Records should account for materials being combined or transformed, with checks between collection, transport, recycling, and production.

Privacy and correction procedures also matter. The published record format includes fields for collector names and coordinates, so institutions should decide which details genuinely need to be public. A record can be marked as revoked, but that does not erase previously published information. Operational continuity also depends on maintaining the devices, application, connectivity, and supporting services used throughout the system.
