Evaluating Ethereum

Understand what using Ethereum can mean in practice for institutions, how it differs from other infrastructure models, and the trade-offs those differences create.

What using Ethereum means in practice

Ethereum can complement existing digital infrastructure rather than replace it. Institutions can use it selectively alongside current systems while retaining authority over their policies, data, and service delivery.

Can institutions use Ethereum while keeping sensitive information confidential?

Yes. Confidential or sensitive data does not need to be stored on Ethereum. They can remain in existing systems, while only the information needed for verification or coordination is recorded on the network. Privacy-preserving methods can also allow specific facts to be verified without revealing the underlying data, further limiting what needs to be disclosed.

What are the cost implications of using Ethereum in digital public infrastructure?

The costs depend on what Ethereum is complementing or replacing. A conventional database managed by a single institution may have lower direct technical costs, but also places responsibility for access, security, and continuity with that operator. When multiple institutions need to coordinate, maintaining integrations and reconciling records can add further costs. Ethereum can reduce some of these coordination costs by providing shared infrastructure maintained by a distributed network.

Recording or updating information on Ethereum requires a transaction, which incurs a network fee. However, reading existing records does not incur transaction fees. Systems can therefore be designed to minimize updates while supporting frequent checks without paying transaction fees each time.

Bhutan's National Digital Identity (NDI) system illustrates this approach. According to an October 2026 report by the Geneva Research Lab for Digital Impact, the system relies primarily on reading existing records, with minimal updates written to Ethereum. New credential issuers and credential types are recorded on the network, while most credential verification happens without incurring transaction fees.

For transactions that do incur fees, a 2026 study by researchers at Rutgers University found median transaction fees of approximately US$0.012 on Ethereum's network in the first quarter of 2026, alongside a benchmark of US$0.0015 across three layer 2 networks, which are systems built on Ethereum that can process transactions at lower cost. Actual fees vary by activity and network.

Institutions should therefore assess the full lifecycle costs, including development, integration, maintenance, resilience, and dependence on individual service providers.

What are the environmental implications of using Ethereum?

Ethereum previously relied on proof-of-work, a consensus system that used energy-intensive mining to agree on network updates.

In 2022, Ethereum transitioned to a new consensus system, proof-of-stake. This reduced the network's estimated electricity consumption by approximately 99.95%. A 2026 study by the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance estimates Ethereum's annual electricity consumption at 7.87 GWh, roughly equivalent to the combined annual electricity consumption of 2,900 average UK households.

Despite its relatively low electricity requirements, Ethereum processed over 524 million transactions in 2025, with additional activity on networks built on Ethereum. Electricity consumption does not increase in direct proportion to transaction volume.

Does using Ethereum mean giving up institutional control?

No. Using Ethereum does not require institutions to relinquish authority over policy, eligibility requirements, compliance, access controls, or operational decisions. Ethereum can provide shared infrastructure for coordination and verification while institutions continue to set the policies, requirements, and operational arrangements for their own services.

How Ethereum compares with other public blockchains

Public blockchains are not all designed and governed in the same way. These differences can have important implications for institutions. The comparisons below examine how Ethereum’s approach differs from other public blockchains.

Resilience

How widely can a software failure spread?

Ethereum

  • Supported by multiple independently developed software implementations that are written in different programming languages and follow the same network rules
  • This diversity reduces reliance on a single codebase and the risk of widespread disruption from a defect in one implementation

Other public blockchains

  • Some rely more heavily on one dominant software implementation or closely related versions
  • A shared defect could affect a larger part of the network

Distributed control

Who can participate in the network’s ongoing operation?

Ethereum

  • Participation does not require approval from a central authority
  • Anyone meeting the publicly documented technical and economic requirements can help operate the network and verify transactions

Other public blockchains

  • Some assign responsibility for verifying transactions to a predefined, elected, or otherwise limited group
  • Coordination may be more direct, but this concentrates operational responsibility and influence among fewer participants

Governance

Who has the authority to change the rules?

Ethereum

  • No single organization can impose changes
  • Changes to network rules can be proposed, reviewed, and debated through an open community process

Other public blockchains

  • Some give a foundation, committee, or other defined group more direct authority over network changes
  • Decisions may be faster or more coordinated, but this creates greater dependence on that governing body

How Ethereum compares with private blockchains

Comparing Ethereum with private blockchains involves more than assessing technical capabilities. The arrangements underlying shared infrastructure can also have important implications for institutional autonomy and long-term continuity.

Network operation

Who can take part in operating the network?

Ethereum

  • Participation does not require approval from a central authority
  • Anyone meeting the publicly documented technical and economic requirements can help operate the network and verify transactions

Private blockchains

  • Network operation is limited to organizations or individuals approved under the governance arrangements
  • Continued participation depends on those responsible for granting and maintaining access

Independent verification

Who can independently verify the shared record?

Ethereum

  • Public record can be independently verified without relying on a single institution or provider
  • Institutions can run their own software to check that transactions and changes to the shared record follow the network’s rules

Private blockchains

  • Verification may be limited to approved participants or depend on infrastructure operated by a consortium
  • Independent verification depends on the network’s governance, access arrangements, and continued availability

Distribution of authority

Who controls the network and how it is used?

Ethereum

  • The network is operated by independent participants, without any single institution controlling its shared rules
  • Institutions retain authority over their own policies, access requirements, and operational decisions when building systems on Ethereum

Private blockchains

  • Designated operators or a governing body have authority over participation and network changes
  • Continued access and operation depend on the governance arrangements and cooperation of those responsible for the network

Operating costs and continuity

What is required to keep the network operating over time?

Ethereum

  • The network is maintained by independent participants, allowing institutions to use shared infrastructure without establishing and maintaining a separate blockchain network
  • Main costs typically arise from developing and operating applications and supporting infrastructure

Private blockchains

  • Ongoing investment is required for infrastructure, security, maintenance, and operations
  • Without sustained financial support, institutions may need to assume operating responsibilities or migrate their systems and records
